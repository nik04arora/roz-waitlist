## SIP vs mutual fund: the short answer

A mutual fund is an investment. An SIP is a way to put money into that investment.

When you invest in a mutual fund, you buy units of a scheme whose money is invested according to a stated objective. Depending on the scheme, its portfolio may hold shares, bonds, money-market instruments, gold-related assets, units of other funds, or a combination of assets.

A systematic investment plan, usually called an SIP, is an instruction to invest an amount into a chosen mutual fund scheme at regular intervals. Monthly is common, although the available dates, frequencies and minimum amounts depend on the scheme and platform.

Asking “SIP or mutual fund?” is therefore similar to asking “monthly payment or the thing being purchased?” You need to choose the mutual fund first. SIP describes how and when you keep buying its units.

## What is a mutual fund?

A mutual fund pools money from investors and invests it through a scheme with a defined objective and portfolio strategy. The scheme—not the SIP—determines what you own and which investment risks you take.

Indian mutual fund schemes include several broad categories:

- **Equity schemes** primarily invest in shares and can experience substantial short-term price movements.
- **Debt schemes** primarily invest in bonds and other debt securities. They can still face interest-rate, credit and liquidity risks.
- **Hybrid schemes** combine asset classes, commonly equity and debt, in proportions described by the scheme.
- **Index funds** aim to track a stated market index rather than have a manager actively select every holding.
- **Solution-oriented and other schemes** include categories such as retirement funds, children’s funds, exchange-traded funds and funds of funds.

The category name alone is not enough to decide whether a scheme suits you. Read its investment objective, portfolio, Riskometer, expense ratio and exit-load rules.

## What is an SIP?

An SIP is a recurring investment facility offered by mutual funds. On each scheduled date, your instalment buys units of the scheme at the applicable net asset value, or NAV.

If the NAV changes, the number of units purchased changes too. The same instalment buys more units at a lower NAV and fewer units at a higher NAV. This repeated purchasing is often called rupee-cost averaging.

Rupee-cost averaging does not guarantee a favourable return or prevent a loss. Your result still depends on the chosen scheme, market movements, costs, the NAVs at which units are bought and sold, and how long you remain invested.

For a fuller explanation of instalments, NAV and costs, read [what an SIP is and how it works](/learn/what-is-an-sip/).

## SIP and mutual fund compared

| Question | Mutual fund | SIP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is it? | An investment scheme | A recurring investment method |
| What does it determine? | The portfolio, objective, costs and underlying risk | The amount, frequency and dates of recurring purchases |
| Do you own it? | You own units of the chosen scheme | You do not own an “SIP” separately |
| Can its value fall? | Yes, the value of scheme units may rise or fall | The SIP schedule does not prevent the units from falling in value |
| Is a return guaranteed? | No | No |
| What is the alternative? | Another investment or mutual fund scheme | A lump-sum purchase, another permitted transaction method, or no purchase |

The most important distinction is this: **the fund determines where your money goes; the SIP determines when additional money goes into it.**

## A simple ₹5,000 example

Suppose Priya chooses a mutual fund scheme and starts an SIP of ₹5,000 per month.

| Month | SIP instalment | Illustrative NAV | Units purchased |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| January | ₹5,000 | ₹50 | 100.00 |
| February | ₹5,000 | ₹40 | 125.00 |
| March | ₹5,000 | ₹62.50 | 80.00 |
| **Total** | **₹15,000** | — | **305.00** |

The SIP is the three-payment schedule. The 305 units are Priya’s investment in the mutual fund scheme.

If the latest NAV is ₹45, those units are worth ₹13,725. If it is ₹55, they are worth ₹16,775. The SIP itself did not produce the gain or loss; the value changed because the units of the chosen scheme changed in value.

This simplified illustration ignores expenses, taxes, exit loads and operational rules. It is not a return forecast.

## Why “SIP returns” can be misleading

People often search for the “return of an SIP,” but an SIP does not have a return on its own. The return belongs to the mutual fund units purchased through the SIP.

Two investors can invest the same monthly amount for the same period and receive very different outcomes if they choose different schemes. Even investors in the same scheme can have different returns if their instalment dates or redemption dates differ.

Before comparing return numbers, check:

1. Which mutual fund scheme was used?
2. What dates and amounts were invested?
3. Are the figures annualised or absolute?
4. Do they include every instalment and the correct cash-flow timing?
5. Are costs and taxes reflected?

Past performance is not a promise, and a high historical return does not make a scheme suitable for your goal.

## Does every SIP have the same risk?

No. An SIP in an equity fund, an SIP in a debt fund and an SIP in a hybrid fund may all use the same monthly payment method, but the investments underneath them are different.

An SIP does not convert market-linked investing into a deposit. It does not provide capital protection, and missing the word “lump sum” does not make the chosen scheme low-risk.

Check the scheme’s current Riskometer and understand what drives its value. SEBI describes the Riskometer as a tool for showing a mutual fund scheme’s risk level. The displayed level can change when the portfolio’s risk changes.

## Should you choose the fund or the SIP first?

Choose the goal and suitable scheme before choosing the transaction method.

Start with these questions:

1. **What is the money for?** Give the investment a goal rather than starting because a fund is trending.
2. **When will you need it?** A scheme should match the time available and the consequences of a temporary loss.
3. **How much risk can the goal tolerate?** Consider both your emotional comfort and your financial ability to absorb a fall.
4. **What does the scheme own?** Read the objective, category, portfolio and concentration—not only its recent return.
5. **What will it cost?** Review the total expense ratio, exit load and whether you are selecting a direct or regular plan.
6. **How does your investible money arrive?** A monthly SIP may match salary income. A lump sum may match money already available for long-term investing.

Once the scheme fits the goal, choose an amount and method you can sustain without using emergency or near-term money.

## SIP, lump sum, or both?

You do not need an SIP to invest in a mutual fund. A lump sum buys units in one transaction. An SIP buys units through recurring instalments.

If your investible surplus arrives with each salary, an SIP may be practical. If money is already available and genuinely intended for long-term investing, a lump sum gives the full amount market exposure sooner. You can also use both methods in the same scheme, subject to its rules.

Neither method is always better. The fair comparison depends on when the money actually became available. Our [SIP versus lump sum guide](/learn/sip-vs-lump-sum/) walks through that decision with examples.

## Other choices an SIP does not make for you

Starting an SIP leaves several important decisions unresolved:

- **Direct or regular plan:** both invest in the same scheme portfolio, but a regular plan includes distribution costs and therefore generally has a higher expense ratio. A direct plan does not include distributor commission.
- **Growth or IDCW option:** these describe how the scheme handles distributable surplus. IDCW is not guaranteed extra income, and distributions reduce the scheme’s NAV to the extent of the payout and applicable levies.
- **Investment amount:** a platform’s minimum is not automatically the right amount for your budget or goal.
- **Review process:** automation should not mean ignoring changes to your goal, finances or the scheme itself.
- **Redemption plan:** an SIP schedules purchases. It does not decide when or how you should eventually withdraw.

Understand these choices before authorising recurring debits.

## A beginner’s checklist

Before starting an SIP in a mutual fund:

- Define the goal and approximate date when the money may be needed.
- Keep emergency and near-term money outside market-linked investments.
- Confirm that the scheme’s objective, portfolio and Riskometer fit the goal.
- Read the current expense ratio and exit-load terms.
- Understand whether you are choosing a direct or regular plan.
- Choose an instalment amount that your bank balance can support consistently.
- Check the debit date, pause and cancellation rules on your chosen platform.
- Review the plan after meaningful changes in your goal or finances—not after every market headline.

If you need a recommendation based on your circumstances, consider speaking with a SEBI-registered investment adviser.
