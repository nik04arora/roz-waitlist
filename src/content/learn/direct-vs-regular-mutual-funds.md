## Direct vs regular mutual funds: what is the difference?

Direct and regular are two plans of the **same mutual fund scheme**. They have a common investment portfolio and are managed by the same fund manager, but their costs and net asset values, or NAVs, are different.

- A **direct plan** does not route your investment through a mutual fund distributor. It excludes distributor commission and therefore has a lower expense ratio.
- A **regular plan** is routed through a mutual fund distributor. Its expense ratio includes distribution costs or commission paid by the asset management company.

The direct plan is the lower-cost version. The regular plan may include help with understanding products, transactions and ongoing servicing. Whether that support is worth the additional recurring cost depends on the service you receive and whether you can make suitable investment decisions independently.

This choice does not change the scheme's underlying market risk. If the portfolio falls, both plans can lose money.

## Direct and regular plans compared

| Question | Direct plan | Regular plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Underlying scheme portfolio | Common portfolio | Common portfolio |
| Fund manager | Same | Same |
| Distributor involved | No | Yes |
| Distributor commission in expense ratio | No | Yes |
| Expense ratio | Lower than regular plan of the same scheme | Higher than direct plan of the same scheme |
| NAV | Separate NAV, usually higher over time | Separate NAV, usually lower over time |
| Scheme-level market risk | Same underlying portfolio risk | Same underlying portfolio risk |
| Investment decisions | Made independently or with separately paid advice | May include distributor assistance |

Compare plans only within the same scheme and option. Comparing the direct-growth plan of one scheme with the regular-IDCW plan of another mixes several differences and will not show the effect of distribution cost clearly.

## Why do their expense ratios differ?

A mutual fund's total expense ratio, or TER, represents permitted recurring costs charged to the scheme's assets. These can include investment management, administration, custody, audit, registrar and other operating expenses.

The daily NAV is calculated after these expenses. You do not normally receive a separate monthly bill for the TER.

A regular plan includes distribution expenses or commission. A direct plan excludes that component, so its TER must be lower than the regular plan of the same scheme. Fund houses disclose current expense ratios, and they can change within applicable rules. Check the latest figure rather than relying on an old comparison.

### A simple cost illustration

Suppose the regular plan's TER is 1.50% and the direct plan's TER is 0.75% at a particular time. The difference is 0.75 percentage points.

On a purely illustrative average value of ₹5,00,000 for one year, 0.75% equals approximately ₹3,750. This is a simple way to understand the scale of the cost gap; it is not an invoice or return forecast. Mutual fund values and TERs can change, and expenses are reflected through the NAV over time.

Even a modest annual difference can matter when it continues for many years. But comparing cost alone does not tell you whether an investor will select an appropriate scheme, maintain a suitable asset allocation or avoid emotional decisions.

## Why are the NAVs different if the portfolio is the same?

Each plan calculates a separate NAV because each bears a different expense ratio. With less deducted for distribution costs, the direct plan generally retains more value. The gap between its NAV and the regular plan's NAV can widen gradually.

The direct plan's higher NAV does **not** mean it is more expensive or that fewer units make it worse. NAV is the value of one unit, not a valuation score. What matters is the percentage change in your investment after costs, not whether one unit begins at ₹20 or ₹200.

Do not compare the number of units between the two plans without also comparing their NAVs and total values.

## What support can a regular plan include?

A mutual fund distributor may help with:

- explaining mutual fund product features and documents;
- completing and tracking transactions;
- maintaining folio, bank and nominee details;
- providing statements and service support;
- reviewing whether existing holdings still serve their stated role; and
- helping an investor understand market events without reacting impulsively.

The actual service varies. Paying through a higher expense ratio does not guarantee comprehensive financial planning, unbiased advice or a particular level of attention. Ask the distributor what support is included, how often it is provided, which fund houses or schemes they distribute and how they are compensated.

## When might a direct plan fit?

A direct plan may fit if you can:

- define your own goals and asset allocation;
- research suitable scheme categories and portfolios;
- understand the Riskometer, expense ratio and exit-load terms;
- compare funds without chasing recent performance;
- complete and monitor transactions independently; and
- remain disciplined during difficult markets.

You can also use direct plans while separately paying a SEBI-registered investment adviser for personalised advice. In that arrangement, the advice fee is paid to the adviser rather than embedded as distributor commission in the mutual fund plan.

Choosing direct only because it costs less can be a false saving if you then buy unsuitable funds, collect overlapping schemes or repeatedly switch based on recent returns.

## When might a regular plan fit?

A regular plan may fit someone who values accessible transaction and product support and is comfortable paying the higher recurring expense for it.

Before deciding, ask:

1. What specific help will I receive?
2. Will the distributor explain risks and costs as clearly as potential benefits?
3. How will the plan be reviewed when my goal changes?
4. Is the support ongoing or mainly provided at the time of purchase?
5. Does the service justify the cost difference for me?

“I am a beginner” is not enough by itself to prove that regular is suitable. Similarly, “direct is cheaper” does not prove that a do-it-yourself approach is suitable. The real trade-off is recurring cost versus the value of the support actually delivered.

## Mutual fund distributor vs investment adviser

These roles are often confused in everyday conversation, but they are not identical.

| Question | Mutual fund distributor | SEBI-registered investment adviser |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main role | Distributes mutual fund products and provides related assistance | Gives personalised investment advice under SEBI's adviser framework |
| Typical payment | Commission from mutual fund distribution | Fee paid directly by the client |
| Mutual fund plan commonly involved | Regular plan | Direct plan may be recommended |
| What to verify | AMFI Registration Number, or ARN | SEBI registration number |

A friendly title such as “financial adviser” or “wealth manager” does not tell you the person's regulatory role. Ask directly: “Are you acting as a mutual fund distributor or a SEBI-registered investment adviser, and how are you paid?” Verify the answer using the relevant registration details.

## How can you check which plan you own?

Look at the full scheme name in your fund-house account statement, consolidated account statement or investment platform. A direct holding should normally include the word **Direct** in the plan name. A regular holding may show **Regular**, or may not include Direct.

Do not rely only on the app's brand or the fact that you invested online. An online platform can facilitate either type of plan. Check the exact scheme-plan name and the latest TER shown by the fund house.

If the statement is unclear, ask the fund house, registrar or platform to confirm the plan and whether an ARN is attached to the holding.

## Should you switch from regular to direct?

Do not switch only because you discovered that direct has a lower TER. First separate two decisions:

1. **Future instalments:** stopping a regular-plan SIP generally stops only future purchases. It does not move units already owned.
2. **Existing units:** moving existing units from regular to direct is normally processed as a redemption from one plan and a purchase into the other.

That switch may create a taxable capital gain or loss. Current exit-load rules, lock-ins and transaction timing may also matter. Different schemes and purchase lots can have different conditions, so read the latest scheme documents and obtain tax advice for your situation before acting.

Also decide how scheme selection and monitoring will happen after distributor support ends. Lower cost is useful only when the new arrangement still gives you a suitable, maintainable plan.

## Common mistakes to avoid

### Assuming direct and regular are different funds

They are different plans of the same scheme. The portfolio and manager are common; the expense ratios and NAVs differ.

### Choosing from NAV alone

A higher direct-plan NAV is not evidence that it is expensive. Compare returns in percentage terms and use matching dates, plans and options.

### Assuming direct means free

A direct plan still has an expense ratio. It removes the distribution-cost component, not every cost of operating the scheme.

### Assuming regular includes complete financial advice

The level of support differs between distributors. Understand the service and the distributor's role instead of inferring it from the plan name.

### Switching without checking tax or lock-in rules

A plan switch is not merely a label change. It may be treated as a redemption and new purchase, with consequences that depend on your units and the scheme's current rules.

### Ignoring whether the fund itself is suitable

Plan choice comes after scheme choice. A lower-cost direct plan of an unsuitable high-risk scheme remains unsuitable.

## A practical decision checklist

Before choosing direct or regular, ask:

1. Does the mutual fund scheme suit my goal, time horizon and capacity for loss?
2. What is the current TER of each plan?
3. If I choose regular, what continuing support will I receive?
4. If I choose direct, who will select and review the investment?
5. Can I follow the plan during a large market fall?
6. Have I verified the intermediary's registration and payment model?
7. If I already hold units, have I checked the tax, load and lock-in consequences before switching?
